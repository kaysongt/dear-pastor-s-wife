Raising Whole Children in a Fishbowl

Pastors' kids aren't born rebellious, they're shaped. Here's how to raise grounded, well-rounded children without the pressure of being "the example."

Adapted from Dear Pastor's Wife by May Ijisesan.

I never planned to have "Pastor's Kids." I hoped to have children, of course, but I never intended to raise that brand of child, the PK everyone whispers about. I carried horror stories from my own childhood about pastors' kids who supposedly turned out wayward because their parents were too consumed with ministry to raise them.

Then life happened. I fell in love, married, and one day I was gazing into my newborn son's eyes when it hit me: his father is a pastor, I am a pastor, that makes him a pastor's kid. It was an epiphany that genuinely scared me. How would he turn out? How would we avoid raising a cliché?

That fear turned out to be a gift, because it made me intentional. And intentionality, I have learned, is the whole game.

Rebellious pastors' kids are made, not born

Here is a truth that should both sober and encourage you: the angry, rebellious PK was not born that way. More often than not, such children simply did not get the attention they needed, because their parents poured themselves into ministry at their children's expense. They are a product of nurture, not nature.

That means the outcome is not fixed, and it is largely in your hands, with God's grace. Parents raising children under the pressures of ministry already have some odds stacked against them, so we cannot coast. We have to be prayerful, present, and dependent on God. I could not control everything, but I knew that with a willing heart, God could teach me to parent in a way that was both unique and fulfilling.

Don't make your child "the example"

Some years ago, a church member corrected my son about something, and then added, "Do you not know you are an example to all the children in the church?" I appreciated the correction. I did not appreciate that last part.

That is a crushing weight to hang on a child. My son did not need to stop being himself and perform for everyone else's benefit. You and your husband heard the call; your children should not be forced to carry responsibilities that were never theirs. So make it your aim to raise your child to be the best version of themselves, not a mascot who performs because the world is watching. Yes, the world is watching. But one of your jobs as a parent is to help your child bear that reality, not add to it.

Practically, that means keeping your expectations age-appropriate and culturally appropriate. I have caught myself comparing my son to church members who were older and more mature, until he reminded me the person I was measuring him against was five years his senior. Do not expect a ten-year-old to carry the maturity of a fifteen-year-old. Let your children be children.

Build kindling around their hearts

Kindling is the small, easily combustible material you use to start a fire. You cannot decide for your children to love God, but you can surround their hearts with kindling so that when God sends a spark, it catches easily. A few things that build kindling:

Take them to church. Make belonging to a local body feel natural, not optional.

Live the Word in front of them. What you do speaks louder than what you say. They will see your flaws, so be authentic, and let them also see someone genuinely committed to God.

Listen to them. Do not only talk to your children; hear what they have to say. That is how you learn what lies the enemy is trying to plant, so you can replace them with truth.

Protect their hearts from church hurt. People who were once part of your life (and your children's) may suddenly not be there anymore. As much as possible, especially when they are young, do not let your children absorb your wounds. Shield their hearts so it stays easy for them to believe people are worth loving.

Refuse the comparison trap

The church gives you endless opportunities for what I call competitive jealousy, comparing your child to someone else's. You might feel pressure because you cannot buy the clothes a wealthier family buys, or because your child does not perform the way another child does. But comparison is a thief of peace, and here is what it costs you: while you are busy admiring another child's strengths, you may be completely missing what is unique in your own. Your child may not be gifted in science but wildly creative, and you will overlook that gift entirely if your eyes are fixed on someone else's.

Give your children what you can, and remember that the most valuable investments have nothing to do with money, a culture of God's Word, respect for others, the confidence to believe in themselves, and help discovering their own destinies in God. Children are precious. They must never become collateral damage in the work of ministry.

Reflect

What is one pressure (an expectation, a comparison, a "you're the example" burden) that you can lift off your child this week? And what is one unique gift in your child that you can name and celebrate out loud today?

May Ijisesan
