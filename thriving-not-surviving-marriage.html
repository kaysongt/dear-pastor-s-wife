Thriving, Not Just Surviving: Building a Marriage That Weathers the Weight of Ministry

You don't have to settle for merely surviving your marriage. With intentionality and courage, a pastor's marriage can thrive.

Adapted from Dear Pastor's Wife by May Ijisesan.

In the early years of my marriage, I could go for days without speaking to my husband. When he asked what was wrong, I would say, "Nothing", while a great deal was churning in my mind that I refused to discuss. I am introspective by nature; my instinct is to internalize rather than talk. So I would withdraw, keep malice, and quietly file the offense away in what I can only describe as mental storage space for the things my husband had done. I did not recognize it at the time, but a spirit of unforgiveness was at work, and my attitude was not healthy. I had to change.

I tell you that not to confess my flaws for their own sake, but because I want you to know something: you do not have to settle for merely surviving your marriage. You can thrive. It takes intentionality and courage, but every effort you make toward thriving is an effort to protect what God has joined together.

The marriage stretched you, and that is a gift

Being married to a pastor placed demands on me to grow more vigorously than I might have otherwise. Your choices, your time, the company you keep, even the trajectory of your life have all been shaped by the person you married. That can feel daunting. But growth in one area makes you more capable in every other. So resist the temptation to pine for a different kind of man or a simpler kind of marriage. Understand yours, and make it work for you.

You are teammates, not opponents

Someone once compared marriage to getting into a person's car. The car has a destination, and the goal is to arrive safely without a wreck. God set my husband as the driver, with his hands on the wheel. He can (and should) invite my participation, but to avoid a crash, I do not wrestle the steering wheel from his hands. That is what submission means for me: coming under the mission so it can succeed. There is a mission in your marriage that is bigger than either of you.

But hear this clearly, submission never means your opinion does not count. A husband who treats you as though your voice does not matter is not walking in God's design for marriage. Both people contribute in thought, conviction, and the leading of the Holy Spirit. When you disagree, talk it through; more often than not, the two of you together arrive somewhere better than either of you would have alone. And when you still disagree, you can pass the test of a gracious heart: choosing not to be disagreeable even when you believe you are right. We submit out of trust, not in a man, but in the God who sees our hearts.

You will get better at whatever you practice

Here is a principle that changed my home: you get better at what you practice. If you habitually fight over your disagreements, you will become an expert fighter. If you consistently try to talk things through, you will become an expert communicator. My husband had his own growing to do, learning to listen without assuming, and without turning every conversation into a sermon. It takes two people growing for a marriage to work. But you can only control your half. So develop your communication. Do not bottle up what a simple, honest conversation could resolve.

Guard the friendship, and stay one team over money

Finances cause more strife in ministry marriages than almost anything. When most of the family's income comes through the pastor, and the money feels tight, resentment can quietly take root. Please do not let it. You are on the same team, it is the two of you against the world, not against each other. Acknowledge that he is doing his best, and then ask what you can bring to the table. If your church responsibilities allow, there is nothing wrong with working outside the ministry; it can bring in income, widen your world, and introduce you to new people. I loved the years I spent in the marketplace while serving in the church.

And pay attention to budgeting. When God called me to full-time ministry, I had to shed an expensive lifestyle I had grown used to. It took two long years of overspending before I got it under control, and here is what surprised me: eventually I felt rich again, not because my income had jumped, but because my spending was finally in order. Give yourself time, but keep improving. Finances are never a good enough reason to fall out of agreement with your husband. He is the best prayer partner you will ever have; you rob yourself of heaven's benefits when you war with him.

A word on safety

Thriving is built on a foundation of honor and safety. If your home is not safe, if you are enduring physical or emotional abuse, please know that your life is more precious to God than any image that needs protecting. Reaching for spiritual and professional help is not disloyalty; it is wisdom. Do not suffer in silence.

Reflect

What is one conversation you have been avoiding with your husband? This week, choose to talk it through instead of storing it away. Before you do, pray: "Lord, as I open my heart to my husband, I trust You. I trust that as I obey You, You have my best interests in mind."

May Ijisesan
